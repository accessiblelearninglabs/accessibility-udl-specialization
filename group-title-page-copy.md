Accessible Learning Labs. Written to match the scale-and-consequence voice of the eleven area pages, and pointed at what excellence in each area makes possible.

## 1. Services
`/services`

**Built once,**
***carried by every cohort after.***

The reach of a design decision is easy to underestimate. A syllabus written well is read by every student who takes the course. A platform configured well is used by everyone on it. A team that has learned to see barriers keeps seeing them long after the engagement closes.

That is the leverage available here. Access is not a service to be repurchased each year; it is a property that can be built into a programme and left there.

**Accessibility review →**
What is working, what is not, and a defensible order of work — so effort goes where it removes the most for the most people.

**Inclusive course and curriculum design →**
Access as structure rather than correction, in programmes designed to hold the range of people who will actually arrive.

**Inclusive facilitation and capacity building →**
The capability moved inside the organization, so the practice continues without an external hand and improves without one.

Each engagement is grounded in a knowledge resource that sets out its method in full — the decisions it turns on, the competencies it calls for, and the scholarship behind it.

## 2. Grounding
`/grounding`

**A barrier you can name**
***is a barrier you can move.***

The World Health Organization estimates that 1.3 billion people — about one in six — experience significant disability. The most consequential thing a designer can learn about that number is that very little of what it describes is fixed.

Disability studies gives a practitioner the language to locate a barrier in the environment, where it can be changed, rather than in a person, where it cannot. That single shift turns a large category of problems from permanent conditions into design questions with answers.

The rest follows from it: knowing what assistive technology genuinely does for someone, reading the categories of disability barrier-first, using prevalence figures honestly, communicating well, and working alongside disabled people as colleagues — a partnership that reliably produces better design than either party reaches alone.

The grounding every later decision is made inside.

## 3. Rights, duty and accommodation
`/rights-duty-and-accommodation`

**A right that holds,**
***and is rarely needed.***

Canadian law is unusually clear here: the duty to accommodate is owed to a person, and it is real. The best version of this work keeps that duty entirely intact and makes it necessary far less often.

Both halves matter. Every barrier removed in advance is a request someone did not have to make, an explanation they did not have to give, and attention they get to spend on the learning instead. And when a request is still needed — as it always will be, because human variability has no outer edge — it meets an institution with the capacity to answer it well, rather than one exhausted by cases that good design would have prevented.

Statistics Canada found that in 2022, more than seven in ten people with disabilities had met at least one of twenty-seven kinds of accessibility barrier. Those barriers have been named and counted, which is precisely what makes them addressable.

The legal and ethical architecture within which access decisions are made and defended — the domestic duty, the essential-requirements question, disclosure as a design variable, and the international instruments above them.

Written in support of the accessibility advisors, accommodation professionals, and disabled people whose expertise this field rests on, and never as a substitute for individual accommodation practice.

## 4. Designing for variability
`/designing-for-variability`

**Designed for the range,**
***better for everyone in it.***

Flexibility built for one group of learners has a long history of turning out to serve almost everyone. Captions were built for deaf viewers and are now used in every noisy room. A course with more than one way in helps the student returning after fifteen years away, the one working night shifts, and the one who simply thinks better in a different medium.

This is the part of the field where the gains are widest and the resistance is lowest, because nobody has to be persuaded that the result is better. Variability is not disability — it is the ordinary spread of any group of people, and it is predictable enough to design for from the start.

The cost objection is usually a production question rather than a design one, and production questions have solutions.

Designing at scale for learner variability, onboarding across wide ranges of prior experience, engaging adults who are there by choice, and making flexibility affordable to produce.

## 5. Standards and technical practice
`/standards-and-technical-practice`

**It works the first time,**
***on the technology people already have.***

There is a particular quality to a thing that simply works — no workaround, no email, no waiting. Someone opens the document and reads it. Someone joins the session and participates. Nothing about the moment is remarkable, which is the point.

Standards make that repeatable. They give a distributed group of people a shared floor, a common vocabulary, and a way to hold a vendor to something specific. Testing with the technology people actually use is what carries a standard the rest of the way into practice.

And the field can learn. Deciding in advance what would count as evidence that a barrier came down turns accessibility work from an assertion into a finding — one that transfers to the next course, the next team, the next institution.

Triage for existing course inventories, assistive technology in practice, physical and hybrid learning environments, and evaluating whether a design reduced barriers.

## 6. Institutions, governance and change
`/institutions-governance-and-change`

**One decision upstream**
***reaches every course downstream.***

A clause in a procurement contract governs every programme that will ever run on that platform. A template shapes every document made from it. An instructor who changes how they design carries it into every course they teach for the rest of their career.

This is the highest-leverage work in the field, and the least visible. It is also durable in a way individual fixes are not: a decision written into how an organization operates survives the departure of the person who argued for it.

The Accessible Canada Act and the Accessible British Columbia Act both require organizations to publish accessibility plans and to invite feedback on them — which means the mandate already exists and the question is what to do with it. Naming who decides, writing accessibility into the terms a vendor is held to, and developing instructors in ways that change practice rather than awareness: ordinary organizational craft, applied where it compounds.

Procurement and vendor accountability, institutional accessibility planning as a change programme, faculty development that changes practice, and closing the gap between knowing and doing.

## 7. Sectors and participation
`/sectors-and-participation`

**Who is in the room**
***changes what the field can do.***

A disabled clinician brings knowledge to patient care that cannot be taught in a lecture. A study designed so disabled people can take part produces findings that actually describe them. Health information written to be usable is care that reaches the person it was meant for.

These are settings where access work pays out twice — once for the learner, and again for everyone that learner will go on to serve.

Clinical placement is a learning environment and a care environment at once. Patient safety and a student's access are both real and both serious, and holding them together is analytical work with genuine answers, not a trade-off to be split. Getting it right widens who is able to enter a profession, and a profession that includes disabled practitioners is measurably better at serving disabled patients.

Access in clinical and placement education, accessible health and patient education, and accessible research participation and knowledge products.

Offered to learning design colleagues entering this work, and never in place of the clinical educators, placement coordinators, and disabled practitioners who hold it.

# Title page copy — 7 groups

Accessible Learning Labs. Craft standards from the Fieldwork set, adapted: the functional
rule and the standing test apply in full; the counts are scaled to the artefact.

---

## 1. Services
`/services`

**Built once,**
***carried by every cohort after.***

Three engagements.

A review establishes what is blocking whom, measured against WCAG 2.2 Level AA and against
how people work with the material, and returns a prioritised order of work.

A design engagement builds access into the structure of a programme: outcomes, assessment,
materials, platform.

A capacity engagement moves the practice inside the organization, so the next course does
not need an external hand.

Each is grounded in a published knowledge resource setting out its method in full: the
stages, the decisions it turns on, the competencies it calls for, and the sources behind
them.

## 2. Grounding
`/grounding`

**A barrier you can name**
***is a barrier you can move.***

The World Health Organization estimates that 1.3 billion people, about one in six,
experience significant disability.

Where a barrier is located decides what can be done about it. Locate it in a person and the
response is a referral. Locate it in the environment and the response is a design change,
available to anyone with authority over that environment.

Held here: models of disability and what each one licenses; the categories of disability set
out barrier-first; what assistive technology does; what prevalence figures can and cannot
support; professional communication; and partnership with disabled people in design.

The frame every later decision is made inside.

## 3. Rights, duty and accommodation
`/rights-duty-and-accommodation`

**A right that holds,**
***and is needed less often.***

In Canada the duty to accommodate is owed to a person and is not discharged by having a
process.

Statistics Canada found that in 2022, 27% of people aged 15 and over had one or more
disabilities, and that more than seven in ten of them had encountered at least one of
twenty-seven defined kinds of accessibility barrier. Each of those twenty-seven has been
named and counted, which is what makes it possible to design against.

Proactive design reduces how often the duty is invoked. It does not replace the duty, and
was never intended to. Human variability has no outer edge, some barriers are the thing an
assessment is built to measure, and the duty is owed to a person. Both practices are
permanent, and both are skilled.

The domestic duty, the essential-requirements question, disclosure as a design variable,
conditions that fluctuate, and the international instruments above them.

Written in support of the accessibility advisors, accommodation professionals, and disabled
people whose expertise this field rests on, and never as a substitute for individual
accommodation practice.

## 4. Designing for variability
`/designing-for-variability`

**Designed for the range**
***of people who arrive.***

Prior experience in an adult cohort spans years. Reading speed, working memory, language
background, hours available, and the device in someone's hand vary across any group, and
they vary predictably.

Designing for that spread from the outset is ordinary instructional work: more than one
route through the material, more than one way to demonstrate an outcome, and a first hour
that holds both the beginner and the person returning after a decade.

Variability and disability are distinct. Variability is the ordinary spread of any group of
people. The distinction is load-bearing: designing well for the spread does not discharge
what is owed to an individual, which is why this group sits beside Rights, duty and
accommodation rather than inside it.

Learner variability at scale, onboarding across wide ranges of prior experience, engaging
adults who are there by choice, and making flexibility affordable to produce.

## 5. Standards and technical practice
`/standards-and-technical-practice`

**Conformance is the floor**
***the rest of the work stands on.***

WCAG 2.2 Level AA is the reference standard in Canadian practice. The levels named in the
Accessible Canada Act and the Accessible British Columbia Act sit within it.

A standard gives distributed teams a shared floor, a common vocabulary, and something
specific to hold a vendor to. It is checkable by someone who was not in the room, which is
what allows accessibility to be governed rather than negotiated.

Conformance testing does not establish that a person can complete a task, and the W3C is
explicit that no tool determines conformance on its own. Testing with the assistive
technology in actual use, and stating in advance what would count as evidence that a barrier
came down, carries a standard the rest of the way.

Triage for existing course inventories, assistive technology in practice, physical and
hybrid learning environments, and evaluating whether a design reduced barriers.

## 6. Institutions, governance and change
`/institutions-governance-and-change`

**One decision upstream**
***reaches every course downstream.***

A clause in a procurement contract governs every programme that will run on that platform. A
template shapes every document made from it. An instructor who changes how they design
carries it into every course they teach afterward.

The Accessible Canada Act and the Accessible British Columbia Act both require organizations
to publish accessibility plans and to establish a process for receiving feedback on them.
The mandate exists. What turns a published plan into changed practice is ordinary
organizational craft: naming who decides, writing accessibility into the terms a vendor is
held to, and developing instructors in ways that change what they build.

Work at this level is slower than fixing a document. It also survives the departure of the
person who argued for it.

Procurement and vendor accountability, institutional accessibility planning as a change
programme, faculty development that changes practice, and closing the gap between knowing
and doing.

## 7. Sectors and participation
`/sectors-and-participation`

**Who is able to take part**
***shapes what a field can know.***

A clinical placement is a learning environment and a care environment at once. Patient
safety and a student's access are both real, and both are serious.

Working out which requirements are genuinely essential to the profession, and which are
customary, is what keeps that question from being settled by assumption. It is analytical
work with answers, and it decides who is able to enter.

Past the learner, the same question runs on. Health education a patient cannot read is care
that has not arrived. A study a person cannot take part in produces findings that do not
describe them.

Access in clinical and placement education, accessible health and patient education, and
accessible research participation and knowledge products.

Offered to learning design colleagues entering this work, and never in place of the clinical
educators, placement coordinators, and disabled practitioners who hold it.

Section 1 of four

How this landscape is organized

This is the accessibility work of a learning design practice, gathered in one place. It draws on two branches: Accessibility, which concerns access at the level of an individual person and the obligation an institution holds toward them, and Universal Design for Learning, which concerns designing for the predictable range of learners before any individual request is made. The two are related but not the same, and the arrangement here keeps them distinct while showing where they meet.

Resources are arranged in seven groups by what they are for, rather than by which branch they grow on. A group therefore holds resources from both branches where the question they answer is shared. Each resource names its home branch so the distinction is never lost. Groups are given in alphabetical order, followed by the three engagements that draw on them.

Every resource states the bodies of knowledge it rests on and cites its sources by number to the reference list in Section 4. Every resource on this page is practised; where it is marked as in development, the grounding and sources are settled and the written resource is not yet published.

This landscape zooms into two branches of the learning design practice mapped at andrearobin.com, where the trunk is Groundwork, the learning design method. The contributions here that do not appear on that map are made possible by Fieldwork, the research method practised at Accessible Learning Labs.

Two kinds of relationship are given for each resource. Most related is calculated from sources and themes held in common, and surfaces resources that answer similar questions from different groups. Connected resources are the direct relationships recorded on the map — the resources a reader is likely to need alongside this one.

Designing Out the Disclosure Requirement

Disclosure burden as a design variable

Practised · resource in development

Access that is gated behind disclosure asks a person to name a private matter to someone holding authority over them, and the documented costs of that request are real: anticipated stigma, delay, and for some, a decision not to ask at all. This resource treats disclosure burden as something design can measure and reduce — why a flexible deadline requiring an email is a disclosure event and a built-in buffer is not — while leaving the accommodation record itself where it belongs, with the office that holds it.

Designing for Episodic and Fluctuating Conditions

Program architecture for conditions that vary

Practised · resource in development

Attendance policy, deadline architecture, cohort progression, and leave-and-return pathways — the structural layer of a problem that individual accommodation handles with difficulty because most systems assume that access requirements are stable. It is program and policy design, offered in the knowledge that disabled people and experienced advisors hold far more understanding of these conditions than the design literature yet reflects.

The Interactive Process in Learning Settings

A shared duty, not a request and a ruling

Practised · resource in development

Canadian human rights law treats accommodation as a process with obligations on every party to it, including the person seeking accommodation, and it sets limits on what may be asked of them. This resource covers who initiates, what good faith requires on each side, what documentation of the process is distinct from documentation of the person, and the points at which the process stalls: no response, an unworkable offer, or a request handled as a complaint.

What is asked of a person before they are believed

Peer Mentorship as Access Infrastructure

Trained peers between the student and the office

Practised · resource in development

Most accommodation systems have two parties, the student and the office; the daily work of access happens between them, often with no one assigned to it. This resource designs the middle: peer mentors trained in integrated learning and technical support, matched to incoming students with accommodations, with clear limits on what a peer decides and a route back to the advisor for what a peer cannot. It is the peer-based support that current accessibility-office role descriptions ask for, written as program architecture.

Reading Disability Documentation

What a report can establish, and what it cannot

Practised · resource in development

A psychoeducational or medical report is evidence about a person at one point in time, produced by instruments that each have a norm group, a margin of error and a purpose. This resource covers what standard scores, percentiles and confidence intervals do and do not say; the difference between a diagnosis, a functional limitation and a barrier in a particular course; what a clinician’s recommendation is evidence of; and how the person’s own account stands beside the report. It is measurement literacy for anyone who has to read documentation, using the same validity reasoning that is used to judge an assessment. The reading of an individual’s file, and the decision that follows from it, rest with the accommodation office.

The Advising Conversation

Trauma-informed, developmental and student-centred

Practised · resource in development

The first conversation about access often follows years of being disbelieved, and it shapes whether a student comes back. This resource sets out that conversation as a designed encounter: how safety, choice and transparency are built into its structure; how to ask about barriers without asking a person to prove themselves again; how the student leaves more able to describe their own access requirements to an instructor; and where advising ends and counselling begins. It draws on needs-assessment interviewing in learning design and on student development theory.

Responding to Distress and Making a Referral

What the person in the room does first

Practised · resource in development

A conversation about access sometimes arrives at a crisis: a student in acute distress, at risk of harm, or about to withdraw. This resource treats the response as a pathway that is designed in advance: recognising what is in front of you, responding calmly, knowing the limits of the role, making a warm referral to counselling, health or emergency services, and recording what was done. It also covers what a teaching team needs to know before the moment comes, and the support the person who responded needs afterwards.

Reasoning Through an Accommodation Request

Illustrative cases, and the record a decision leaves

Practised · resource in development

Invented cases, worked from first contact to a recorded decision: what the barrier is, what the documentation and the person’s own account establish, what the course or program actually requires, which options were considered, and why one was offered. Each case shows the record the reasoning leaves behind, including what is written down, what is kept out and who may see it, because a decision that cannot be explained later is hard to defend and hard to learn from. No case is drawn from a real person. These are teaching cases; decisions in real cases rest with accessibility advisors, accommodation offices and legal counsel.

Accommodated Exams and Assessment Logistics

Time, setting, format and integrity

Practised · resource in development

Extended time, a separate setting, a reader or scribe, assistive technology, an alternate format, a deferred sitting: each changes the conditions of an assessment, and each has to be delivered on the day. This resource covers when a condition is part of what is being measured and when it is not; what the research on extended time does and does not show; how an accommodation carries into group, lab and applied exams; and how integrity rules protect a result without treating accommodation as suspicion. It then sets out the logistics that decide whether an approved accommodation actually happens: booking, training invigilators, what students and instructors are told and when, and the calls that have to be made in the moment when the clock, the technology or a person’s health fails mid-exam.

Funding Routes for Disability-Related Supports and Equipment

Cost as an access barrier

Practised · resource in development

An approved accommodation can still depend on money: an assessment the student has to pay for, equipment, software, a tutor, an interpreter. This resource maps the routes open to post-secondary students in British Columbia, including federal and provincial grants for services and equipment, equipment loan programs and institutional funds, with what each covers, who is eligible, what documentation each asks for and how long each takes. It also treats the application as a design problem: where the forms are themselves the barrier, and what a program can do so that a student is not waiting on funding in order to begin.

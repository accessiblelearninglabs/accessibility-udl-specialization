Section one of seven

Foundations

What this section is for: naming the bodies of knowledge the resource stands on, so a reader can judge the work before they take it up.

Write this section last

Foundations are what you find you relied on, not what you meant to. Draft the process, decisions and outcomes first, then come back and name the disciplines those sections actually drew from. Anything you name here must appear again in Sources with a citation. If it has no citation, it is not a foundation — cut it.

Use between four and ten cards

Order them by how load-bearing each one is, heaviest first. Fewer than four usually means the node is a sub-topic of something else on the map. More than ten usually means two nodes have been folded into one.

Section two of seven

Process

What this section is for: showing how the work is actually done, in the order it is done, so the reader can locate themselves in it.

Use numbered stages only when order carries information the reader needs. If the steps can be taken in any sequence, they are not stages — move them into Decisions and delete this section's numbering. Five to eight stages is the workable range.

1 Stage title, as an action The condition that makes this stage necessary +

Two or three short paragraphs. Write what the practitioner does, in the active voice, in the order they do it. Name the judgement call inside the stage — the thing that separates doing this well from doing it at all.

This stage produces
  • A named artefact, not an abstraction
  • Another named artefact
2 Every stage keeps the same shape Prose, then a "this stage produces" list +

Consistency across stages is what lets a reader skim. If one stage has no tangible output, that is a signal it belongs inside an adjacent stage rather than standing alone.

This stage produces
  • A decision that later stages depend on
3 Stages are open by default in the linear file The same content, laid out differently +

In the linear template these are ordinary headings and paragraphs. Write the text once, paste it into both files. Never shorten it for one format.

This stage produces
  • Two files whose content is verifiably identical
Section three of seven

Decisions

What this section is for: surfacing the choices that shape the work, phrased as questions a practitioner has to answer for themselves.

Each entry is a heading, a question, and an answer. The question is the point — it is what the reader carries back to their own project. Write questions that a thoughtful practitioner could answer either way; if there is only one defensible answer, it is a rule, not a decision, and belongs in Process. Four to eight entries.

Short name for the decision

The question, written the way a practitioner would ask it?

What hangs on the answer, and what each way of answering costs. Two to four sentences. Do not resolve it for the reader unless the evidence genuinely resolves it — then say so and cite the source in Sources.

Short name for the decision

The question, written the way a practitioner would ask it?

Keep the answers roughly even in length. A decision with three times the prose of its neighbours reads as the only one that matters.

The question, written the way a practitioner would ask it?

If two decisions always get answered together, they are one decision. Merge them.

Section four of seven

Competencies

What this section is for: stating what a person must be able to do to practise this, so the resource can be used for hiring, scoping, or self-assessment.

Write each as a capability, not a topic: "can separate the competency from the method of demonstrating it," not "understands assessment." Six to ten entries. Each one should map onto something in Process — if it does not, either the competency is unused or the process is incomplete.

Capability, phrased as a verb

One or two sentences describing what someone with this capability produces that someone without it does not.

Capability, phrased as a verb

Avoid dispositions — "is empathetic," "is detail-oriented." They cannot be observed, taught, or assessed, so they cannot be competencies.

Capability, phrased as a verb

Name the tools or standards the capability requires, if any. That is what makes it checkable.

Section five of seven

Outcomes

What this section is for: separating what the work produces soon from what it changes over time, so neither is oversold.

Two columns, because the two kinds of claim carry different evidential weight. Short-term outcomes are things you can point at within a project. Longer-term change is what the evidence suggests follows, and it should be phrased with the hedging the evidence actually supports.

Within the project

  • A concrete outcomeWhat it looks like when it has happened, in one sentence.
  • A concrete outcomePrefer outcomes someone else could verify without taking your word for it.
  • A concrete outcomeThree to six entries.

Over time

  • A longer-arc changeMatch the hedge to the evidence: "tends to," "in the studies available," "reported in."
  • A longer-arc changeAnything here that is a claim about effect needs a citation in Sources.
  • A longer-arc changeThree to six entries.
Optional block

What this does not do. A short paragraph naming the limits of the work. This is the most-read paragraph in most resources of this kind, and leaving it out is the fastest way to lose a sceptical reader.

Section six of seven

Reflection

What this section is for: letting a reader locate their own situation against the resource, and leave with a next step rather than a score.

Three to five questions, each a radio group. Options run from least to most established practice, left to right. Every option carries a data-score from 1 to 4; the average selects one of the four bands written below. The band text lives in the HTML, not the script, so you write it and nothing is generated.

Section seven of seven

Sources

What this section is for: making every claim in the resource checkable by someone who does not trust you yet.

Group sources by the foundation they support, in the same order as Foundations. Give each one a kind label so a reader can tell a standard from a study from a practitioner text. Link only to a stable public address — a publisher page, a DOI, a standards body. Do not link to a paywalled PDF as the sole address for a claim.

Before publishing

Open every link in this section. A resource whose citations rot is worse than one with fewer citations, because it invites a trust it can no longer carry.
